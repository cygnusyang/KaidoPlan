How do I export a delivery report for a client?

A delivery report is not a screenshot of a task list. It is an argument with four parts, and every number in it should be reproducible from the plan file the client can be handed.

Updated 2026-11-17 · by cygnus, the developer of KaidoPlan

Four sections, in this order

Order matters because each section depends on the one before it. A report that opens with percentages has already lost the argument.

  1. The computed finish date. Not the date someone hoped for, and not a range invented for safety. The number the schedule produces from the current durations and edges.
  2. The chain that produces it. The critical path, named by task. This is the load-bearing section: it converts "trust me" into "these three tasks decide the date, and here they are".
  3. Open violations. Conflicts, cycles, tasks outside the project window. A report that hides an open conflict is a report that will be contradicted next week, by the person who reads the plan themselves.
  4. Variance against a baseline. Planned versus actual per milestone, so that "on track" is a measurement rather than an adjective.

What a section 1–4 report looks like

MilestoneBaseline finishComputed finishVariance
Import pipeline accepted2026-11-212026-11-210 days
Reconciliation report signed off2026-11-272026-11-28+1 day
Handover to operations2026-12-042026-12-08+4 days

Then the sentence that makes the table useful: "The four days are on the reconciliation chain — the fixture set took a day longer than planned, and the handover rehearsal is serialised behind it." That is a report. The table alone is a spreadsheet, and the percentage-complete column that usually accompanies it says nothing about the date.

Note what the middle row does to the last: a one-day slip upstream becomes four days at the end, because the tasks after it have no slack. This is the single most common thing a client does not believe until they see the chain.

Where each number comes from

Every figure in the report is one command away, which is what makes it auditable rather than asserted:

Report lineCommand
Computed finish datekaidoplan schedule compute --file plan.kaido

The --csv form is the one worth knowing about if the client wants the numbers in their own tracker: it writes the per-task variance rows to a file instead of printing them, and the project-level totals stay identical to what the report shows.

Preview before you send

If you want the habit before the report, the five-minute delivery check is the same four numbers in a smaller loop. The app's workbench previews the delivery report before export, which is the cheapest place to catch a number that contradicts the text you wrote around it. Export is unlimited during the trial; on the free tier it is three reports a month, with the rest of the planning workbench intact.

The baseline habit that makes reports cheap

Take a baseline at the start of each reporting period — kaidoplan baseline create --name "Sprint 12 start" --file plan.kaido. From then on the report is a comparison, not a narrative written from memory.

It also removes the most common argument in a delivery review: whether the date changed or was always wrong. With a baseline the question has a diff answer. Without one, both sides are describing their recollection of a document, and the person who is more senior usually wins.

Two rules for baselines, learned the hard way:

  • Baseline after you accept a change, not before. A baseline taken mid-negotiation freezes a schedule nobody agreed to.
  • Keep them. A baseline you deleted is a variance you cannot compute, and the period it covered becomes unauditable retroactively.

What to leave out

  • Percentage complete, as a headline. Forty tasks at 70% tells the reader nothing about the date, and it invites the question "so are we on track", which is the question the report exists to answer without being asked.
  • Task counts. "38 of 52 done" is a volume metric. The date is set by the zero-slack chain, which is usually two or three tasks.
  • Any number you cannot reproduce. If a figure in the report does not come from a command in the plan, it is a claim wearing a table's clothing, and the first technical reader will find it.

When the number is bad

The report is most valuable on the week the date moves, and that is the week people rewrite it. The structure that survives a bad number is the same structure as always, with one added paragraph at the top:

The delivery date is now 8 December, four days later than the baseline of 4 December. The change is on the reconciliation chain: the fixture set took a day longer, and the handover rehearsal is serialised behind it. To recover the four days, either the rehearsal moves in parallel with sign-off, or the fixture scope drops to the two formats that matter.

Date, cause, options. No apology and no optimism. A client who receives that in week two trusts the next report; a client who receives "still on track" for six weeks and then a four-week slip does not, and the arithmetic was available the whole time.

Keep the plan in the repository and the report has a source you can point at — a commit, a baseline and a diff. That is a stronger position than a slide deck nobody can audit, and it is the difference between reporting a schedule and defending one.
